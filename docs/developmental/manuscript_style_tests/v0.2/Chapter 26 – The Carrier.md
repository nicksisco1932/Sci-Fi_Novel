They appeared on the manual board first: maintenance marks from lines the biomechanic had never seen answer together. One showed a sealed boundary. Another showed a route opening. Three vanished as soon as the attendant tried to hold them on screen.

The biomechanic cut the display feed.

The marks continued on the next panel.

One line held between the branching patterns:

MAINTENANCE AUTHORITY / NETWORK ISOLATION / HOST COHERENCE LOW.

The biomechanic read it twice.

"What does that mean?" Cassian asked.

"The system recognizes a shutdown mechanism. It entered through maintenance authority and is asking weakly connected nodes to isolate from one another."

"A switch?"

"A switch buried in a handshake. It gets a system to trust the channel, then makes the system cut its own connections."

"Why?"

"That part isn't here."

The biomechanic turned the manual slate toward him. "If the network is loose enough, you can break its ability to coordinate before it can identify you as a threat. A civilization can be disabled before it becomes meaningful in a war."

"So it was made for a war."

"It was made to produce that result. That doesn't tell us who made it, who it was meant for, or whether the system is reading it correctly."

The display changed. The line broke into three separate statuses, none of them agreeing.

"Disconnect the local monitor," they said.

The attendant pulled its power.

The wall display remained dark for a breath, then lit with the same branching pattern.

"It's local now," the younger attendant said.

"It's always local somewhere," the biomechanic replied.

They turned the manual return wheel. It would not move.

Cassian leaned his shoulder against the console. His side burned with every breath. Thena lay behind the inner screen, held flat by braces, her face turned away from the lights.

"Can you close it?" he asked.

"The ward return, perhaps. The next junction has already answered."

"How far?"

"I don't know."

"Do you know anything?"

The biomechanic glanced at him. "Yes. It is not contained here."

The younger attendant pressed a palm to the wall, feeling for vibration. "Water pressure is changing in the south line."

The older attendant looked at the ceiling conduits. "No. That's the north cooling branch."

The difference mattered to them. Cassian heard it in the way they moved: not panic, but the need to stop one uncertain thing from becoming two.

The biomechanic took a manual slate from the rack and began writing channel numbers. "Shut the south line only if the pressure crosses the second mark. Leave the north branch open. There are still people on its return."

"It may carry the state," Cassian said.

"It carries water."

"Now it carries both."

The biomechanic did not answer. They handed the slate to the attendant and went to check the isolation screen.

The first message came through a hand terminal at the ward entrance. A child from the enclave had run it down and stood in the doorway, holding the device with both hands.

"The cistern won't open," they said.

The older attendant took the terminal. "Which one?"

"The east one. It keeps saying the line isn't ours."

"Who told you to bring this here?"

The child looked at Cassian.

"No one. I heard he knew before the lights changed."

The older attendant's face tightened. "Who said that?"

"Everyone."

Cassian looked at the child. "I didn't know about the cistern."

"But you knew the lights."

"I saw one thing before it happened. I don't know why."

The child considered him. "That's what knowing is."

The older attendant guided them back into the corridor and closed the door.

The terminal kept sounding its small alarm.

The biomechanic took the device from the older attendant and checked the status. "The east cistern is refusing the local credential. The west valve still works. Send them to the west one. Don't force the east."

"It feeds three rooms," the attendant said.

"Then tell them to ration until we know why it refused."

"And if the west line fails?"

"Then we'll know more than we do now."

The attendant's mouth tightened. They carried the message out.

The terminal alarm stopped.

Another signal arrived from a different direction. A voice, broken into fragments by the old relay. A transit shelter had lost its air circulation. A clinic in the Lower Grid reported its repair bed opening on its own. Someone had seen maintenance lights running along a sealed wall in a district that had not received power in years.

Every account contradicted the last.

One enclave had opened an old passage to reach a trapped family. Another had sealed its doors and cut the water feed. A third had begun stripping a fabrication bay before anyone could determine whether it had actually unlocked.

No one asked Cassian what to do.

Not at first.

Then the messages changed.

They had come from the junction.

He had spoken before the lights.

He had returned from outside.

He had brought the pattern back.

He could see what came next.

Somebody asked him to name the safe route. Somebody else asked if the event had already reached their shelter. The older attendant switched the relay off.

"They are going to make this about you," they said.

"They already have."

"You could tell them you aren't responsible."

"Would they believe me?"

"No."

The biomechanic returned from the other room with a thin sensor pressed to Cassian's temple. "Hold still."

He let them place it.

The sensor warmed against his skin. A faint pulse crossed its display, then split and disappeared into noise.

"What is it?" Cassian asked.

"A local neural read."

"What did you find?"

"A brief response in the temporal region. It doesn't look like a normal signal."

"Can you remove it?"

"I don't know what it is. I won't cut into your brain to find out."

"The quarantine?"

The biomechanic looked toward the partition. "The quarantine pattern is isolated to your temporal lobe so far. I can measure that much. I can't tell what it is doing there, or what happens when it finishes."

"But it is happening."

"Yes."

They took the sensor away.

The younger attendant stood near the open maintenance bay, watching the darkened display. "What about the Voice? Can it tell us where this is going?"

"The Voice sent us here," Cassian said.

"It also sent you to open that valve," the attendant said.

"No."

"You think it knew?"

"I think it knew enough to find this ward. It didn't tell me what would happen."

"How do you know?"

Cassian had no answer.

The ward settled into a long, uneven quiet. Messages came by hand now, each one carried from a different corridor. The east cistern stayed closed. The west line held. Two people argued over whether to open an old transit route; the enclave sent neither of them back with an answer.

Hours passed.

Cassian had not slept. He kept his eyes open until the lights changed to the dim cycle used for the ward's night shift. His ribs throbbed in time with his breathing.

Then the room was elsewhere.

Korin stood beneath a hard white light, older or simply more tired than Cassian remembered. Alyen faced him across a table. Between them lay a narrow strip of dark material, its surface marked by a pattern Cassian could not read.

"If he turns on me?" Alyen asked.

Korin looked at the strip.

"Then you will know what he is."

The image held on Alyen's hand closing over the strip.

Cassian came back to the ward with his fingers clenched around the chair.

The biomechanic stood beside him. "How long?"

"I don't know."

"What did you see?"

He told them.

The biomechanic looked toward the broken terminal. "That was a memory?"

"No. I have never been in that room."

"Was it real?"

"I don't know."

For nearly six hours, no one came to the ward.

Then the older figure entered, rainwater on one shoulder. "A woman from the upper sector is asking for you. Says she has a message from Korin."

Cassian looked at the narrow corridor beyond them.

"Alyen?"

The older figure's face changed by almost nothing. "You know her?"

"No."

The older figure watched him a moment longer, then went back to the door.

The biomechanic did not ask how he knew the name.

The ward door opened without a knock.

The older figure stood aside for the woman in the gray coat.

The lights in the ward dimmed once, then returned.

The woman did not look at Cassian. She placed a narrow strip of dark material on the table.

The same branching mark ran beneath its surface.

Cassian's hand tightened on the chair.

The biomechanic stepped between Alyen and the table.

"No one touches that in my ward."

"Then don't," Alyen said. "I came to tell you Gor is on his way."

The older figure lowered their voice. "Who sent you?"

"Korin."

"And what did he tell you?"

Alyen looked at Cassian at last.

"That I would know what Gor is if he turned on me."

The image Cassian had seen was not complete.

It was not wrong.

The ward door opened without a knock.

The older figure from Below the Map stepped inside. Water darkened one side of their coat. Their hands were empty.

"The east cistern is shut," they said. "West line is working. Two routes are open that were sealed when I was a child. One clinic is turning people away because its beds won't release. The upper grid says nothing is wrong."

"And the watcher?" Cassian asked.

"Alive. Talking. They say Gor came back through the runoff, then went up."

"Where?"

"They don't know."

The older figure looked at Cassian's head sensor on the table.

"They say you saw the door before it shut."

"I saw it after it happened."

"That isn't what they say."

"Then they are wrong."

"Maybe."

The older figure came closer. "Some want you out. Some want you tied down until this stops. A few think you can tell them which routes are safe."

"I can't."

"I told them that."

"Did they believe you?"

"No."

The older figure glanced toward the corridor where Thena lay. "We are moving her to the old pressure ward. It has manual support and no shared bed system."

The biomechanic nodded. "Good. Keep her there. No networked frame."

"And him?"

"He stays here until his ribs stop trying to puncture him."

"The people outside want to see him."

"They can wait."

"Some won't."

The biomechanic's expression did not change. "Then keep them away from my patients."

The older figure turned to leave.

The wall terminal came on again.

This time it showed a line of text in the Hive's administrative font:

CONTAINMENT PRIORITY: CARRIER / LOCAL MACHINE ECOLOGY / EXPOSED PERSONNEL.

The display held long enough for everyone to read it.

Then the terminal wrote over its own message with a second line:

ROUTE ORIGIN UNVERIFIED.

The biomechanic took a manual tool from the wall and broke the screen.

The lights in the bay continued to flicker.

From somewhere far above, a pressure system engaged in a sequence the ward's gauges could not trace.

The older figure stood in the doorway.

"Is that Korin?" the attendant asked.

"It is a containment instruction," the biomechanic said. "That does not tell us who issued it."

Cassian got to his feet. The room tilted and steadied.

"Gor is coming back."

The older figure looked at him. "You saw that?"

Cassian waited for the next image.

Nothing came.

"No," he said. "I know what he does when he has a trail."

The older figure nodded once. "Then we move before he does."

The ward began sealing its outer doors.
