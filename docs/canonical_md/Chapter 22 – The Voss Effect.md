They did not leave the room as guests.

Two watchers took point. The masked one stayed behind Cassian's left shoulder, close enough for the weapon to matter and far enough not to be taken easily. The runner carried the rain-dark coat folded in both hands, as if the missing man might still object to careless handling.

Thena walked beside Cassian with her injured hand tucked against her ribs.

"Still think this counts as shelter?" she asked.

"Conditional shelter."

"That's a polite way to say hostage with errands."

"Yes."

The old figure who had questioned them led without looking back. The enclave opened around them in layers: sleeping alcoves, water drums, storage walls marked in chalk, children moving copper tags from one bowl to another under an adult's low instruction. No one blocked the way. No one pretended not to watch.

Below the Map did not waste curiosity.

They passed through a corridor where the ceiling dropped low enough to force even Cassian to lower his head. The wall on the right had been cleared of soot and patched with panels cut from older signage. Someone had painted over the panels in flat ochre, red-brown, and black.

Not a warning mark.

A mural.

The escort slowed because two residents were dragging a water frame across the passage ahead, and the line compressed around the delay. The old figure stopped at the bottleneck. The watchers stopped with them.

Cassian stopped before the mural because the woman in it had not been painted like the others.

The workers, soldiers, mothers, and children of the descent were rendered in the flat ochre style of Under-Hive memory: faces simplified, bodies narrow, hands exaggerated around tools and ration bowls. But the woman above them was precise. Pale dress. Dark hair. One hand raised beneath a sun the color of bone.

"Maribel Voss," Cassian read from the cracked nameplate.

One of the children sorting copper tags made a small sound in his throat.

The old woman beside Cassian did not look at the child. "That was what The Great Union called her."

Cassian glanced at her. She had come out of an alcove without sound, one shoulder wrapped in a patched gray shawl, hands stained green from stripped wire.

"And what did you call her?"

"That depends on the century."

On the mural, rain had been etched falling around Voss in silver lines. It touched the crowd, the towers, the descent gates. It did not touch her.

"The Voss Effect," the old woman said. "First it meant money. Later it meant weather."

Cassian looked back at the painted rain.

"Money."

"Real estate no one lived in. Shell entities. Disaster trusts. Continuity firms. Federation contracts large enough to hide a province inside." She shrugged. "The usual miracle. Everything vanished and everyone signed the receipt."

Thena's mouth tightened, but she said nothing.

"Weather," Cassian said.

"Acid rain through one hundred thirty-five-degree heat. The phrase was already old by then, so people kept using it. People like old words. Saves them from admitting the thing got worse."

Cassian looked closer. Beneath the official inscription, someone had scratched a second sentence so deeply that the wall had chipped around each letter.

*We were supposed to live lives!*

The old woman followed his eyes.

"They say a man shouted that before they killed him. Tried to shoot her. Maybe he missed. Maybe he didn't. Maybe there was never a gun." She shrugged. "The old stories improve themselves when the dead are unavailable."

"She was assassinated, wasn't she?"

"Yes."

"And then?"

"Then The Great Union remembered it was not united." The old woman touched the lower edge of the mural, where whole sections had been scraped away. "The States of the Willing slaughtered the Minority World-State and called it preservation. Later we called it the Voss Conquest. Later still, the victors killed one another over which version of her had given permission."

Cassian stared at the painted woman, untouched beneath the acid rain.

"She did all that?"

"No," the old woman said. "That is the frightening part. She only taught them the language."

The water frame scraped through the bottleneck ahead.

The masked watcher shifted once, impatient but not surprised by the interruption. The child with the copper tags went back to sorting. Thena gave the mural one last look, then turned away as if the story had been another exposed pipe in the wall: old, dangerous, already known.

Cassian did not move at once.

On the mural, Voss's raised hand had been repainted more often than the rest of her. Layer over layer. Correction over correction. Someone had wanted the gesture preserved long after the face stopped mattering.

"Some say she was an administrator," the old woman added. "Some say coordinator. Martyr. Profiteer. Artifact, if you like stories with clean monsters. Down here, mostly, she is a lesson about what happens when enough people need permission."

"Permission for what?"

The old woman looked past him toward the corridor where the runoff team waited.

"Whatever they were already willing to do."

"Move," the older figure said.

The pause ended.

They went on.

The passage narrowed after the mural and sloped toward colder air. Chalk marks gave way to blade cuts. Children and water frames fell behind them. Ahead, the enclave became work again: watch posts, shuttered lamps, tool racks, quiet hands passing evidence from one person to another without ceremony.

The runner unfolded the torn strip of route cloth as they walked.

"Runoff cut is two turns past the south cistern," they said. "Outer lantern failed three minutes before the coat was found."

"Failed," Thena said, "or was put out?"

"That's why you're walking."

Cassian kept his eyes on the route marks.

Three cuts. One reopened.

A hunter could learn a language by watching where people were afraid to step.

The air grew wetter. Mineral cold pressed through the corridor seams. Somewhere ahead, water moved hard through a channel too narrow for the volume it carried.

The old woman's words stayed behind him with the mural.

Not revelation.

Residue.

A dead language still teaching the living how to excuse themselves.

At the last corner before the runoff cut, the older figure raised one hand.

Everyone stopped.

No one spoke.

Beyond the turn, the outer lantern was dark.

And something had scraped fresh lines into the wall beside it.
