The ward alarm began.

Not a siren. A low repeated tone from the pressure panels. The lights over the sealed bay turned red, then amber, then red again.

The biomechanic stood. "Move the patients to the pressure ward. Manual supports only. Don't reconnect their frames."

The older figure was already in the passage. "The return is pushing through every branch."

"Then close what we can without starving the cooling lines."

"How many?"

"As many as we can keep breathing."

The attendant lifted Thena's stretcher. Cassian tried to follow, but the floor shifted under him. He caught the wall.

The older figure reached for him. "Stay here."

"No."

"You can't walk."

"I can move."

The biomechanic checked his side with one hand, then let him go. "If you fall again, we leave you where you land."

"Understood."

They carried Thena toward the pressure ward. Her eyes opened as she passed him.

"Are you going?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"You always say that when you do."

The attendant adjusted the mask over her face. Thena's gaze stayed on Cassian until the turn took her out of sight.

The old figure looked at the dead key strip still in their hand. "We can't keep you here."

"Because of the carrier?"

"Because people have already started coming for you. Some want answers. Some want to put you down before you bring another thing through their door."

"And you?"

"I want the west line open long enough to move the people who still need it."

The biomechanic walked to a narrow hatch behind the tool wall. A rusted wheel turned under their hand. Beyond it, a vertical shaft carried cold air from above.

"That lift hasn't run in years," Cassian said.

"It runs when the surface exhaust shutters open. They're opening now."

The pressure gauge rattled in its case.

"Where does it go?"

"A maintenance deck outside the city shell."

Not the surface. Not orbit.

The place between the buried Hive and the open air.

Cassian looked back toward the pressure ward.

"Thena stays here."

"She can't travel."

"Will she live?"

The biomechanic paused before answering. "I can keep her breathing. I can't promise more."

Cassian nodded once.

The lift cage descended from somewhere above. Its brakes scraped against the shaft. The door opened onto a platform that smelled of oil and old dust.

They stepped inside.

The older figure stayed behind.

"You can still close the line," Cassian said.

"Not the one that's already open."

The lift rose.

The shaft walls passed in broken intervals, each section separated by a pressure gate and a service platform. The biomechanic kept one hand on the manual brake. Cassian leaned against the cage and let the machine carry his weight.

Halfway up, the first vibration came through the rails.

The biomechanic looked at the gauge.

"Not from our line," they said.

The vibration strengthened. Dust came loose from the shaft joints. The red lights dimmed and returned.

"Launch systems?" Cassian asked.

"Could be."

The next wave carried a sound with it.

It traveled through the lift's metal frame, then through the air duct beside it. A long, rising roar that split into thousands of overlapping thrusts.

The lift stopped.

The biomechanic braced against the rail. "Surface pressure equalizing."

The lift's doors opened onto a platform above the city shell.

The roar hit Cassian through the open hatch.

He had not heard a rocket launch since before the jump. Then it had been one engine, one vehicle, one intention. This sound had no single source. It came from the whole horizon.

Thousands of launches rose from the Earth at once.

Some followed the familiar scheduled ascent patterns, spaced and measured. Others broke from their marked lanes and climbed on trajectories with no visible command path. Each left a stream of white heat behind it. The sky filled with them.

The biomechanic watched the launches without speaking.

"Are those evacuation craft?" Cassian asked.

"Some. Cargo, likely."

"And the rest?"

"I don't know."

The closest stream bent east, not toward the orbital work lanes. Another ship climbed without a visible clearance light, its vector changing twice before the clouds swallowed it.

The network was not giving one order.

It was producing consequences.

Cassian gripped the cold edge of the platform.

The roar receded toward the upper air, but the ground continued to tremble. A line of lights came on across the surface, one after another, then went dark in no pattern Cassian could read.

"We should go back," the biomechanic said.

Cassian looked toward the sky.

"Will that help?"

They didn't answer.

The lift began its descent without anyone calling it.

The biomechanic reached for the brake. The control did not respond. A second set of lights appeared on the shaft wall, leading upward past the lift's route.

The doors shut.

The cage kept rising.

It passed the maintenance deck and continued toward an upper pressure lock.

"Can you stop it?" Cassian asked.

"Not from here."

The lift stopped at a sealed hatch. A manual wheel turned on the other side.

The lock opened.

Beyond it, the air was colder and thinner. The path led to a bare observation shelf outside the Hive city's outer shell, exposed to the weather and the night.

The biomechanic looked at Cassian.

"You don't have to go out."

He thought of the hand on the blue wheel. The crèche's light. The woman closing her fingers around the dark strip.

"I know."

He stepped through.
