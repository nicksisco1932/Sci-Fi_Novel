The outer shelf had no roof.

Wind moved through the opening in the city shell, carrying cold and the mineral smell of rain. The Hive rose behind them in stacked walls, vents, service towers, and windows darkened to keep out the weather. Beyond the shell, the ground fell away toward an open plain cut by transit lines and launch fields.

Cassian stood at the edge of the maintenance deck.

The biomechanic stayed near the hatch. They had not come outside often. Their eyes moved across the pressure gauges set into the wall, checking each one by hand.

"The lift will keep running?" Cassian asked.

"For a while."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

The launch roar had passed into the upper air. It left a tremor in the deck and a heat shimmer above the far fields. Streams still climbed into the sky, thinner now, separated by wide intervals.

Some were expected. Their course lights followed the established gate approaches. Others left from service yards without clearance beacons, carrying no visible crew. They rose, turned, and began to merge with the same lanes as the scheduled ships.

"What are they doing?" Cassian asked.

"Some will be carrying cargo. Some will be carrying people."

"And the others?"

The biomechanic watched a ship change its course twice. "The maintenance state is in their systems."

"To carry it outward?"

"Or because some part of them thinks it should be carried outward."

The answer left room for the machine, and none for certainty.

For a long time, nothing crossed the sky except those climbing trails. The lights dimmed as the vessels passed into high atmosphere. No voice came over the deck speakers. No announcement claimed what had happened or who had caused it.

Cassian rested one hand against the cold wall.

The wall felt steady.

Behind it, the city was not.

The biometric strip the biomechanic had placed against his temple still sat beneath his collar. He could feel its small weight when he moved. It had shown a response in the temporal region, no explanation. The visions came in fragments, then stopped. He could not tell whether they had ended or were waiting for him to look away.

The biomechanic checked his face. "Anything?"

"No."

"No vision?"

"No."

They accepted the answer without calling it good news.

The wait stretched.

Near the twentieth minute, a white flash opened over the horizon.

The planetary gate rings were visible even from below the city shell: enormous structures circling Earth, their surfaces broken into distant segments by cloud and night. They were not stations. They were a system of rings, wide enough to gather a ship's whole approach and hold it through the transition.

A vessel approached the nearest visible arc. Its running lights dimmed as the ring opened. For a second, the ship and the ring seemed to occupy the same patch of sky.

Then the gate flashed again.

The vessel was gone.

There was no plume, no visible break in the sky. Only the closing arc of the ring and the starfield beyond it, briefly bent out of shape.

The deck terminal woke behind them.

The biomechanic turned back toward it. The old display showed a status line in the Hive's standard format:

OUTBOUND CARRIER: ACCEPTED.

Below it, a second line appeared, then held:

RETURN STATE: RECEIVED.

The source field remained blank.

The biomechanic did not touch the screen.

"Is that from the ship?" Cassian asked.

"No local route claims it."

The line changed.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENT SENT.

The source field stayed blank.

Nothing else came.

The old weather scope, mounted beside the terminal, turned toward the Sun. Its filter shutter opened by degrees. The screen took a moment to resolve the glare.

At first Cassian saw only the pale edge of the disk.

Then a narrow glint appeared beyond it, faint enough to be a scratch in the lens. The scope adjusted. A second glint joined it, then another, each too far apart to make a shape.

The display drew a thin line around the Sun.

Not complete.

Not close.

A ring beginning to form.

The biomechanic touched the focus control. The image sharpened just enough to show sails, long struts, and structures still separated by more space than metal. Beyond the first arc, small craft moved between them. A few of the departing ships were already there, arriving through the Sunward gate.

The ring was barely visible.

The launches were not.

They kept rising from Earth. Streams of light crossed the clouds and climbed toward the gate rings. Some followed old schedules. Others carried routes and states that had not existed when their engines started.

No one on the deck cheered.

Cassian thought about the mission that had taken him away from Earth. The ship. The jump. The warning that had come too late to change his choice.

Perhaps he had failed it.

Perhaps the state had made a civilization pause long enough to notice what it was doing.

Nothing had stopped the launches.

He could not tell whether they were answer, escape, or another layer of the same expansion.

The scope's display began to dim. The thin ring remained on the glass a moment longer than the Sun itself.

He waited for the next vision.

It did not come.

The biometric strip held a faint warmth beneath his collar. The wind moved through the open shelf. Behind him, the biomechanic said something he could not make out.

The scope faded.

He waited.

The quarantine, the light, his own sight—he could not tell which had gone first.
