The maintenance valve clicked again below the floor, somewhere past the sealed bay and local return. The biomechanic turned toward the wall as a small change in pressure followed, and the crèche answered with a pulse of light.

The younger attendant reached for the outer screen.

"Leave it," the biomechanic said.

The hand stopped.

The crèche's membrane had thinned along one side. Under it, pale threads gathered around the sample in a pattern that almost repeated the first branching response. Almost.

"It is trying the boundary again," the biomechanic said.

"Can it get out?" Cassian asked.

"Not through the screen."

"That isn't what I asked."

The biomechanic looked to the pressure gauge. "It has reached the local return. The isolation valves are holding."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

Thena lay beneath the support frame, her back held in a rigid cradle. A mask covered her mouth and nose. She had been quiet since they arrived. When Cassian looked over, one eye opened.

The biomechanic checked the braces beneath her shoulders. "She can hear you. Keep her still."

"I am still," Thena said through the mask.

"You are making an argument with your spine."

"It started it."

The attendant adjusted the frame. Thena closed her eye again.

Cassian looked at the crèche. "What happens if the valve fails?"

"The sample reaches a shared line. The shared line touches systems outside this ward."

"How many?"

"Enough that the answer is not useful."

The biomechanic moved to a narrow panel beside the isolation tank. Under a clear cover sat a manual handle, red-painted at the grip and worn smooth near the joint.

"That will sterilize the local return," Cassian said.

"If I pull it before the line changes pressure."

"And the sample?"

"It will be destroyed with whatever part of it has crossed the isolation boundary."

"Could it already be outside?"

"There is no indication that it is."

"That isn't the same as no."

"No."

The younger attendant set a cutting tool beside the panel. "We can sever the return."

"And lose pressure control for the local loop," the biomechanic said. "That risks every patient on this side of the screens."

They returned to the monitor. The crèche pulsed once. A ripple crossed its outer membrane and ran down the two channels of tissue. At the end, the light stopped.

Not blocked.

Waiting.

Cassian pressed his tongue against his teeth. "It wants an answer."

"It may be testing whether an answer is possible," the biomechanic said. "Want is a word for a mind."

"Then what word do you use?"

"Pressure. State. Response."

"And if it responds to the pressure?"

"Then I pull the handle."

The line under the floor clicked a third time.

The gauge shifted by one mark.

The biomechanic set a hand on the red grip.

"You said the structure is isolated," Cassian said.

"It is."

"The line says otherwise."

"The line says it is communicating with the boundary. The valve is closed."

"You don't know what the boundary means to it."

"I know what it means to us."

The biomechanic’s fingers tightened around the grip.

"This ward keeps people alive because every line has a boundary. Water is not air. Cooling is not tissue. The return is not the supply. People built those separations because one failure should not take the rest with it."

"And the system crossed them anyway."

"It has not crossed them yet."

"You don't know that."

"Neither do you."

Cassian looked at the screen. Beyond it, the crèche's pale branches had turned toward the outer wall. Not all of them. Two. One remained fixed on the sample. The other had grown along the membrane in the direction of the return.

The room fell away.

He saw Earth from outside the Hive, dark against a field of cold points. Streams of light climbed from the surface. Farther out, a gate ring flashed white, then went dark. The image held for one breath and vanished.

He had never seen the view.

Cassian's fingers had dug into the chair arms. Sweat ran cold beneath his collar.

"What did you see?" the biomechanic asked.

"I saw Earth. Outside the Hive."

"I am holding it."

"I have never seen it."

No one moved for a moment.

The biomechanic removed their hand from the grip.

"You saw an image. That is all."

"It was clear."

"Clear is not reliable."

"I know."

The older attendant looked at the younger one. Neither asked how he knew what was about to happen.

Cassian closed his eyes and tried to return to the moment before the image. The room stayed. The pipes. The red handle. The membrane. He could not find the path that had led him to the vision.

"It came from the same place as the phrase," he said.

The biomechanic stepped back from the panel. "The state has started changing something in your head."

"Or it was already there."

"Either way, you don't know what it costs."

The crèche pulsed again.

The gauge moved half a mark.

The biomechanic pointed to the red handle. "I pull that, and the isolated sample is destroyed. The return is sealed. Whatever crossed it, if anything, gets treated as contamination. That is the safest decision I can defend."

"And if the sample holds information that lets you stop it?"

"Then we lose it."

"If you let it answer?"

"It may leave this room. It may touch systems I cannot map. It may alter what it reaches. Or it may do nothing beyond this ward. I have no evidence for the scale."

"Then your safest decision could be the one that makes the next decision impossible."

"Yes."

The plainness of it held Cassian longer than an argument would have.

The biomechanic was not asking him to trust them. They were naming the limit of what they knew.

Behind the screen, the crèche's branches shifted.

The room seemed to narrow around the two controls: the red handle that would destroy the sample and the manual return wheel below it, painted blue beneath years of grime. One ended the test. The other opened a line.

Cassian looked at Thena.

Her eyes were open again.

"You don't know what it means," she said.

"No."

"Then don't tell yourself you're choosing for everyone."

"I'm not."

"Good."

She looked away. The attendant adjusted the air mask without asking her to say more.

The biomechanic watched Cassian. "If you open the return, I cannot promise I can close it again."

"Can you keep the test running without opening it?"

"The local loop is losing pressure. The crèche keeps probing the boundary. I can hold this configuration for a short time."

"How short?"

"I don't know."

The valve clicked.

This time the sound came from the panel itself.

The red handle trembled under its clear cover.

The biomechanic stepped toward it.

Cassian pushed off the chair. Pain flared through his side, but he made the three steps to the panel before the attendant could catch him. He put his hand over the blue wheel.

"Move," the biomechanic said.

"I heard you."

"Then you heard that I can't contain it if you open that."

"I heard that you can't promise containment either way."

"You are not making this choice with complete information."

"Neither are you."

His hand closed around the wheel.

For a second he was back in the cockpit, not seeing words, only the pressure of something looking through the destination field. The impossible opponent. The collective weight of a warning with no grammar.

He had carried it here without knowing.

Now he could choose whether to hide it again.

The red handle remained under glass. The biomechanic stood close enough to stop him and had not.

Cassian turned the wheel.

It moved a quarter turn and caught.

The biomechanic reached for the manual lock.

Cassian looked at them.

"I know what I am doing."

"No, you know what you are choosing."

He held their gaze.

The biomechanic took their hand away from the lock.

Cassian turned the wheel the rest of the way.

The return opened.

Nothing flashed. No voice spoke. The crèche did not unfold or reach toward him. A thin, clear flow entered the narrow conduit, almost indistinguishable from the wash already moving beneath the floor.

The ward trembled once.

The lights did not go out.

On the local panel, a row of unlisted maintenance marks appeared. One, then three, then too many to read before the display shifted to another set. The markings were not language. Each showed the same branching form the crèche had grown, translated into lines of status and connection.

The biomechanic stepped close to the screen.

"That is not a reply from here."

The next panel woke.

Then the next.

Some stayed blank. Some displayed a small containment ring. Others showed the branching mark and an open route.

Across the ward, water moved through the pipes. The cooling line took the pressure. The air vents opened and closed with a measured series of clicks.

The old systems were answering each other.

No single answer repeated.

Cassian's vision fractured.

For an instant, he saw a corridor he had never entered. A woman lifted a sealed hand from a panel. A launch bay opened around a ship already under power. A child reached toward a wall as light came through it in a pattern of branching lines.

The images ended.

Cassian gripped the console until his knuckles whitened.

The biomechanic caught his shoulder.

"Cassian."

He tried to answer. His mouth had gone dry.

The crèche dimmed behind the screen. Its output continued through the open return, carried into pipes that ran farther than the ward, beneath people and machines the biomechanic could not name.

Somewhere above them, a gate status changed.

On the wall terminal, a message appeared in the Hive's ordinary typeface:

ROUTE STATE UNKNOWN.

The words vanished before anyone touched the display.

Then the ward began to fill with other people's lights.
