The ward had no sign.

Its entrance was a service door with three layers of paint and a hinge that had been replaced more recently than the wall around it. The route marker had gone dark two turns back. Cassian kept the sequence in his head as the stretcher carrying Thena scraped along the narrow passage.

The older figure had sent two people with them. One pushed Thena. The other kept close to Cassian, ready to catch him if his legs gave out again.

"How far?" Cassian asked.

"The door is there," the runner said.

"That isn't how far."

"It is all I know."

The runner stopped before the painted door and knocked once near the lower hinge.

Nothing answered.

They knocked again, this time with the side of a fist against the upper panel.

A slot opened at eye level.

The person behind it looked at the stretcher first. Then at Cassian.

"Who opened the route?"

"We don't know," the runner said.

The slot closed.

The door unlocked from inside.

The ward beyond smelled of wet stone, iodine, and warm metal. It was larger than the corridor suggested, built through two connected rooms and a maintenance bay. Pipes crossed the ceiling in bundled runs. Some were marked in new paint. Others had been tagged with small metal plates that carried old standards and newer handwritten corrections.

No one welcomed them.

Someone called for the stretcher. Two attendants moved Thena to a narrow platform under an articulated frame. A third pointed Cassian to a chair.

"I can stand."

"Then you can fall standing," the attendant said. "Sit."

Cassian sat.

The biomechanic came from the rear bay with a pair of stained gloves in one hand. Their hair had been cut close to the skull. A dark streak of repair gel marked one sleeve. They looked at Cassian's ribs, then at the runner.

"This isn't one of mine."

"The route opened," the runner said.

The biomechanic stopped.

They looked at the wall terminal beside the entrance. Its screen was blank, but a thin status line still glowed under the casing.

"It opened from the north trunk?"

"West cooling line," Cassian said.

The biomechanic looked at him again. "Who told you that?"

"The wall did."

They crossed to the terminal and touched the casing without waking it. Their gaze moved once over the blank screen, then to the repair frame beside Cassian's chair.

"How long ago?"

"Minutes."

The biomechanic turned to the attendants. "Separate the intake logs. Keep this ward off the shared board. No uplink. No auto-report."

One attendant hesitated. "It came through a maintenance route."

"I know what it came through." The biomechanic pulled on the gloves. "That is why I said no shared board."

They touched two fingers to the side of Cassian's neck. A flat diagnostic strip unfolded from the chair and pressed lightly under his jaw. Its surface warmed.

"Name."

"Cassian Rho."

"Age?"

"Four centuries, give or take."

"Not what I asked."

"I don't know."

The biomechanic glanced at the readout. "The frame can work with not knowing. Hold still."

The machine extended three fine contact threads toward his side. One stopped at the broken skin below his ribs. Another found the edge of a deep bruise. The third hovered over a thin, old scar beneath his sternum.

Cassian's muscles tightened.

"Those are repair filaments," the biomechanic said. "They are not going to take anything you can spare."

"People say that before taking things."

"Then don't look away."

He watched.

The first filament entered the wound. A coolness spread under his skin, followed by a pressure that moved deeper than the cut. A second filament reached the bruise. The third remained hovering.

Across the room, Thena's breathing hitched as the frame shifted under her. One attendant adjusted the support beneath her shoulders.

"Can she feel her legs?" Cassian asked.

"She can feel pain," the biomechanic said.

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

They did not look away from the monitor.

The repair threads drew back from Cassian's wound.

The coolness stopped.

The attendant nearest him leaned in. "It lost contact."

"No," the biomechanic said. "It withdrew."

The three filaments folded into the chair. A fourth emerged from the frame, wider and dark at the tip. It touched the edge of the cut, then recoiled so sharply its casing clicked against the rail.

The lights over Cassian dimmed.

The repair frame shut down.

Every other frame in the ward remained on.

The biomechanic looked at the control panel. "Isolate this station."

"Already isolated."

"Isolate the local tissue loops."

The attendant moved to the wall and turned a manual valve. Somewhere under the floor, something heavy engaged.

The repair organisms nearest Cassian withdrew into their housing. Not scattered. Not dead. Their movement had the precision of a hand closing around a tool.

The biomechanic pulled off one glove and pressed a control beneath the chair. A narrow drawer opened. Inside lay a length of pale membrane suspended in nutrient gel.

"What is that?" Cassian asked.

"A sample carrier."

"From me?"

"If I can keep it from reacting."

They adjusted the chair's contact threads and drew a small amount of fluid from the wound. The sample entered the membrane. For a moment, nothing happened.

The biomechanic watched its surface.

"You recognize it," Cassian said.

"I recognize the quarantine behavior. I have never seen it execute."

"The Voice sent us here."

"I know."

"How?"

The biomechanic placed the sample carrier in a steel cradle. "The route arrived in the old Verran maintenance cadence. I have seen the same request pattern recur in dead systems over the last few years. Small repairs. No sender. Sometimes the request is timestamped seconds before the fault. Those old junctions used entanglement and tunneling, or names we gave to something we never learned to measure properly. It could be early detection. It could be a narrow look ahead. It has never given enough warning to stop much."

"You knew it was the Voice?"

"No. I knew something had learned to ask for maintenance without having permission to ask. Your route carried the same fault in the same place."

The membrane tightened around the sample.

The attendants stepped back.

The biomechanic moved between the cradle and the other patients. "Close the inner screens. Nothing leaves this bay."

"If it is a contamination event—" one attendant began.

"Then the last thing we do is let the ward decide that for us. Close them."

The screens came down, sealing the maintenance bay behind clear, flexible partitions. The steel cradle opened. Threads of repair tissue lifted from the membrane and reached for one another, making a hollow cup around the sample.

It did not resemble any machine Cassian knew.

It resembled something trying to grow a machine.

The biomechanic watched as the cup thickened at one end and thinned at the other. A membrane drew itself across the opening. A second layer branched beneath it, white threads dividing and rejoining as if searching for a shape they had not yet found.

"What is it building?" Cassian asked.

"An interface."

"For what?"

"For whatever made the repair system withdraw."

The structure flexed.

Cassian felt the word before he heard it.

Stop the expansion.

It did not arrive as a voice. The phrase rose from the same damaged place where Alyen's patterns had lodged, but its edges were different now: separate, ordered, complete in a way the remembered words had never been.

His fingers closed around the chair's arms.

The biomechanic looked at him. "What?"

"I know that phrase."

"From the encounter?"

He nodded.

The membrane in the cradle pulsed once. A fine ridge formed along one side, then divided into two branching paths. The response repeated, but not in the same order.

The biomechanic studied the branching pattern. "That isn't sentence structure."

"What is it?"

"A state exchange. Challenge, response, boundary. It is trying to establish what it is connected to."

"It's a message."

"Maybe. It is not a sentence."

"Then what does it say?"

"I didn't say it says anything."

They leaned closer to the partition. The crèche's outer membrane darkened where its branching tissue met the sample. Small pale nodes appeared under the surface, each one brightening and fading at a different interval.

"Verran?" Cassian asked.

"People say Verran when they mean half the living-machine infrastructure on Earth. It's a lineage and compatibility family, not a single company. Elias Verran's original work is only the root. This crèche comes from one of the early descendants."

"And you don't know what it will do."

"I know what it did. It withdrew. It built an isolated test environment. It is growing whatever it needs to continue."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the one I have."

The crèche contracted around the sample. One of the pale nodes remained lit.

The biomechanic turned to the attendants. "No shared water return. No cooling exchange. Shut the secondary air loop and keep the scrubbers local. If anything in this room starts printing a duplicate status, break the panel."

The younger attendant stared at the structure. "That could contaminate the ward anyway."

"It could."

"You should destroy it."

"I know."

The biomechanic said it without looking away from the crèche.

Cassian looked at the membrane. "You want to destroy it?"

"I want to know whether destroying it would stop what it has already begun. I don't know that."

"Then why keep it?"

"Because someone sent you along a route built from dead systems, and my repair ecology recognized a state it has never seen. Because it is building an interface instead of consuming the sample. Because if I destroy it before I understand which, I may only remove the last thing here capable of containing it."

The answer was not reassurance. It was a list of dependencies, each one unresolved.

The crèche opened along its upper seam.

A thin transparent film stretched over the gap. The pale nodes brightened together. One pulse traveled through the branching tissue and stopped at the boundary of the cradle.

The biomechanic stepped back.

"No one touches the outer line," they said. "No one authorizes a relay. No one answers it outside this room."

Cassian looked at the light held in the sample.

"What happens if it completes the exchange?"

The biomechanic met his eyes.

"I don't know."

Across the ward, Thena tried to move and stopped with a sharp breath.

Cassian turned toward her.

The crèche pulsed again.

Somewhere in the wall, a maintenance valve clicked once.
